A New Adventure

It’s been a while.

The world out there is full of unexplored jungles, lost pyramids, and cursed temples that are just waiting for an unprepared explorer to slip up. Collapsing floors, creaky bridges, poisoned darts, fire jets, and rolling boulders… You can expect all

that, and even more.

But if I know you, and I’ve seen my fair share of people like you, a few obstacles and a handful of deadly traps won’t be enough to stop you. Of course not, you have your eyes set on the treasure. Wherever this journey may lead you, you will rise to the challenge.

So, get up now, on your feet. Pack your bag and grab your hat. Gather your maps, and take your pistol too, for good measure. There’s no turning back now.

Adventure is back.

Pulp Adventure Genre Book

adventureis a pulp adventure game inspired by the classics of the genre, from the immortal Indiana Jones, to The Mummy (1999). In adventure, the Players take on the role of brave Adventurers in search of an invaluable treasure.

adventureis the first Genre Book in the outgunnedline, andyou can play it either standalone or as an expansion to outgunned.

Director’s Cut

adventureuses a game system called Director’s Cut, a set of mechanics using small pools of six-sided dice to determine the outcome of the Adventurers’ actions. When rolling dice, your goal is not to score the highest or lowest possible number, but to roll two-, three-, and four-of-a-kind.

EVERYTHiNG YOU NEED TO KNOW

Do I need outgunned to play? No. You can play adventure as a standalone game, without any other rulebook. But if you also have the outgunned corebook, you can enrich the gaming experience with additional mechanics and Roles.

Who are we? One of the Players will be the Director of the game, they will be tasked with setting the scene of the world and interpreting supporting characters, extras, and enemies. The others will play as Adventurers, people determined to find their treasure.

Where are we? The game is set somewhere in a cooler version of our World, where the untamed wilderness hides remnants of forgotten civilizations, and mystery lies just around the corner.

When are we? The game is set sometime between 1920 and 1940, when the World was still wide, different, and unpredictable. Depending on the needs of your story, you can choose a set date, or settle for a vague “between the two Wars”.

What’s the goal? To find your treasure. Whether it’s cash money or eternal life, real or metaphorical, the most important thing for you is the treasure, not the journey!

How many Players do we need? Besides the Director, the game is designed for a group of 3-4 Adventurers, but can easily be played with two or five Heroes without needing any changes.

What dice do we roll? You’ll need a handful of 6-sided dice. There are special Adventure Dice designed especially for this game, but they are not required.

How many Sessions? The game is designed for short Cinematic Campaigns of 5-8 Sessions and, given its quick and easily accessible nature, is perfect for oneshots. For longer campaigns, I recommend you string together several shorter stories to create a Cinematic Saga of sorts. Like Die Hard 1, 2, 3, etc.

Genre Book

adventureis the firstGenre Bookfor the cinematic action roleplaying game outgunned. This book is not your typical expansion, but a brand-new format.

But before looking at the details, let me ask you a question: do you already own the outgunned corebook?

Yes, I have the outgunned corebook .

You can treat adventure like a mega-expansion, a rulebook that changes some of the standard outgunned mechanics and enriches your gaming experience with additional Roles, Character options and mechanics. Everything you need to bring grandiose pulp adventures to outgunned is contained in this book, including a rich chapter dedicated to the Supernatural .

But there’s more! adventure offers simplified rules for combat scenes, while also adding new concepts such as traps, runs, and multiple dangers. You will be able to choose which tools to use for your sessions, picking freely from the corebook and this genre book. All fully compatible. Customize your gaming experience!

For your convenience, all the rules previously included in the corebook are compiled in Section 2: BASICS OF THE GAME . Veterans like you can skip that chapter (even though, I must say, it has been enhanced with new examples that could come in handy).

No, I do not own outgunned.

The good news is that adventure is completely standalone.

You don’t need any other book to play, not even the outgunned corebook. In this genre book, you will find everything you need to create Adventurers and set off on your search for the Treasure.

But if you want to make use of the detailed tools to manage adrenaline-paced action scenes, consider that the outgunned corebook contains all the advanced mechanics to manage chases, enemies, firearms, and ranges.

Not to mention 10 unique Roles, such as the Sleuth, the Agent, or the Spy, 18 Tropes, and a slew of Traits for both Adventurers and their Enemies. All perfectly compatible with adventure.

How to Play

To play adventure you’ll need some friends, some paper, pencils, and a handful of 6-sided dice. One of the Players will take on the role of Director and be tasked with describing the world and portraying all of the extras, supporting characters, enemies, and the Rival. The others take on the roles of Adventurers , men and women of action, ready to risk it all in order to reach the Treasure before their Rival.

The Director creates interesting and thrilling scenes and the other Players take turns acting as their Adventurers.

When the Adventurers face something challenging, or when they run risks and something threatens to go wrong, we rely on the roll of a handful of dice to determine the outcome of the Adventurers’ actions.

Game Terms

  • ♦ Adventurer: The protagonists of adventure are called Adventurers. In other outgunned rulebooks, Characters are called Heroes, but “Hero” and “Adventurer” are fully interchangeable terms for the purposes of the game.

  • ♦ Dice: This game uses six-sided dice or d6s. The custom Adventure Dice make it easier for you to spot successes, but they are not required to play. If you don’t have them, you can use any d6 you already own.

  • ♦ Turn: During a turn, each Adventurer takes one action. Once everyone has acted, you move on to the next turn.

  • ♦ Scene: A small snippet of the story, like a dinner party, a chase, or a night spent keeping watch.

  • ♦ Session: The occasion when your group of friends comes together to play is called a game Session.

  • ♦ Shot: An adventure that usually unfolds during a single session.

  • ♦ Cinematic Campaign: Connected Shots related to the same adventure create a Cinematic Campaign.

The Pillars of Adventure

When playing adventure, you are practically playing through your own adventure movie.

Even if you are not an expert of the genre, you will soon realize that going on a great adventure is not hard at all. You can start by following these three recommendations.

Adventure never stops

When you manage to break free, the Rival has already left for the Treasure. You look around and find an old seaplane. By the looks of it, it can barely manage to take off. There is no time to warn the authorities or drive to the airport, you just have to do it.

When adventure knocks on your door, it never gives you a clear destination. The only thing you can be certain of, is that you’ll have no time to dawdle. In any adventure movie worthy of this name, the characters are constantly propelled forward, they must always get by with the limited resources they have on hand, and deal with an onslaught of adverse circumstances. There is no time for first-class tickets, no time to wait for help. There is barely enough time to make rushed decisions and risky choices.

That’s the Adventurer’s way!

The journey is the Treasure

You're barely holding on, literally and metaphorically at the end of the rope. The Treasure glimmers nearby, but try as you might, you can’t get to it. “Let it go!” yells your friend while grabbing your hand “It’s not worth losing you”. The Treasure is the horizon toward which Adventurers travel at breakneck speed, but it is not the focus of the story. The most important part of an adventure is adventure itself. The thrilling journey that takes you to distant cities and unexplored jungles in search of clues.

When you set off on an adventure, try not to focus only on the Treasure and its history. Instead, give room to the Adventurers, to their growth and journey, and to the relationships they forge along the way.

Mystery just around the corner

When you enter the secret room, you feel a shiver run down your spine. For a moment, you could swear you are not alone… But it must have been your imagination.

An ancient temple has waited for centuries, protected by surprisingly advanced traps that somehow still work. Who takes care of the maintenance?

A diary emerges from the ocean. Its contents rewrite History as we know it. But what is the truth?

A dangerous sarcophagus enshrines the subject of a terrible curse. Or maybe just an ancient unknown disease?

Soon after setting off on your adventure, you start to notice strange events, stuff that just eludes all logical explanations, and maybe it’s better like that. There are some mysteries that should remain mysteries. Not all questions need answers.

Show don’t tell

The best movies don’t just tell you about things, they show things with facts and actions!

You don’t need long back-stories and heart-wrenching tales. What truly matters is what’s written on your Sheet, and what happens in-game.

If your Adventurer fought in the war, you don’t need to say it to the other Players while

eating pizza. It’s much better if you show them turning over

an old faded photo. If a fellow Adventurer says “Your brother and I were ride or die”, that’s how things are. At least, that’s their version of the facts. Apparently, you have a brother now!

A Good Day for an Adventure

In 1922, a guy named Howard Carter started digging in the desert and unearthed the lost tomb of Pharaoh Tutankhamun. Hundreds of pounds of solid gold with great historical value that propelled the whole World to set off on adventures, in search of Treasure.

That was the beginning of a new golden age of modern exploration. An age made up of zeppelin flights and rowdy jazz, that culminated in the 30s with the esoteric expeditions of those nutty Nazis.

A handful of years when scheduled flights allowed normal people to cross oceans and continents, but when the World had yet to be explored. United, connected, but still new and uncontaminated.

Adventurer Sheet

Adventurers are men and women of action who face great risks because they’re on a quest to find an invaluable treasure. Their lives are always hanging by a thread, as they face terrible odds.

Some Adventurers are hat-wearing professors who punch Nazis while trying to save the World. Others are hopeful youngsters in search of their past, or librarians too curious for their own good. Others still are heirs from wealthy families, ready to risk it all for a treasure. Some are even tomb robbers and smugglers who end up involved in something greater than themselves.

But they all have something in common.

If you want to be a great Adventurer, you must follow these 3 rules:

Your greatest Treasure: You are looking for a treasure. Maybe you're trying to get it back to its rightful owners, or to find a new home for it in a museum, or maybe you simply want to get rich or learn the truth about a mystery from your past. You don’t necessarily need to know about the treasure when the game begins, but as soon as you get a chance, it will become your only goal. Nothing is more important than the treasure.

Take risks: You must face the adventure with determination, tenacity, and a pinch of recklessness. The road to the treasure is paved with traps and adversities, but you are ready to do what it takes in order to succeed. No matter if you were collecting dust in a library up until yesterday, now that the treasure is within reach, you cannot back down. Take risks and never look back.

Do the right thing: For every professor with a firm sense of justice there are at least two grave-robbers ready to betray their companions for a handful of gold coins. After all, this is a dangerous line of work. You don’t need to be respectable, but deep down, you are one of the good guys. Even if you make mistakes and sometimes act like a real piece of crap, in the end, your heart is always in the right place.

Personal Data

This is everything you know about your Adventurer. Anything else, you’ll have to live through or learn during the game. We don’t take lengthy backstories here, only facts, actions, and surprising plot-twists.

  • ♦ Name: Your name.

  • Jason O'Donnell

  • ♦ Background: Your background gives you access to information, to contacts, or to specific knowledge.

  • Former Soldier

  • ♦ Age: Young, Adult, Old.

  • Adult

  • ♦ Catchphrase: Your tag line, your creed, the words that guide you when it’s time for action. Basically, your entire identity summed up in a sentence. I’ve had worse!

  • ♦ Flaw: Nobody is perfect. Your flaw is the thing that keeps tripping you up throughout your adventures.

  • I’m afraid of spiders

Your Background can prove useful when you need to gather information or ask for help. For example, Jason can call on his past as a soldier to contact an old brother in arms, or to recognize the uniform of an enemy soldier.

Your Catchphrase is a clear indication of how you should portray your Adventurer, and the Director can also use it as a benchmark for when to reward you with a Spotlight . For example, if Jason manages to find a way to succeed when everyone else was already writing him off, he certainly deserves a Spotlight!

Your Flaw , on the other hand, is something you’ll have to deal with or overcome on your adventure. You can use your Flaw as a guide on how to play your Adventurer, and the Director can call your Flaw into play to impose -1 to a relevant roll. For example, Jason is afraid of spiders, which means he could be reluctant to venture into a cave full of cobwebs or lose his cool when a tarantula drops on his head.

Adventurers of All Ages

You can choose your Adventurer’s age between:

  • ♦ Young: You’re a kid or a teenager, who has yet to learn their trade. If you’re a Professor, you're probably an assistant or a young genius, and if you’re a Captain, I seriously doubt you have a pilot's license. Anyway, you still have a long way to go.

  • ♦ Adult: You’re in your best years and in top shape. You are at the perfect age to set off on an adventure. You may have some mileage, and your back might be starting to ache, but you're still going strong!

  • ♦ Old: You can try to hide it, but the years (and miles!) are beginning to take their toll. You’re no spring chicken, and are beginning to understand it. If you’re a Technician, back in your day, things were built to last, and if you're a Daredevil, you certainly have many memories and scars to share. But your work is not done yet.

Most Adventurers are Adults. As such, Adult Adventurers receive no advantages and no disadvantages. If you choose to be an Adult Adventurer, all you need to do is write that on your Sheet, and you’re set. On the other hand, if you want to play a Young or Old Adventurer, you need to follow these rules.

Young Adventurers

Young Adventurers can choose only 1 Feat from their Role instead of 2. In exchange, they gain an exclusive Feat only available to them: Too Young to Die . Additionally, Young Adventurers start the game with 2 Luck, instead of 1. Ah, the enthusiasm of youth!

Old Adventurers

Old Adventurers can choose one additional Feat among the ones available through either their Role or Trope.

However, they start the game with 2 Lethal Bullets in their Death Roulette instead of 1. When you reach a certain age, you need to be careful.

Additionally, Old Adventurers can choose to start the game with one Experience of their choice. We will talk more about Experiences in Section 7: TO ADVENTURE!

Roles

The Role of an Adventurer is a key feature that tells you what kind of person they are, what they can do, and probably also how they spent their life before this journey. This is a crucial choice!

The first thing to do when creating your Adventurer, is choosing their Role:

  • ♦ The Daredevil

  • ♦ The Guardian

  • ♦ The Captain

  • ♦ The Hunter

  • ♦ The Heart

  • ♦ The Star

  • ♦ The Professor

  • ♦ The Technician

  • ♦ The Scoundrel

  • ♦ The Smuggler

When you choose a Role, you gain 1 point in an Attribute and 1 point each in 10 Skills. Then, you can choose 2 Feats from the list of 6 for the Role.

Additionally, your Role also gives you some starting Gear , and suggests Backgrounds , Catchphrases , and Flaws you can use as inspiration to fill in your Personal Data.

If you ask me, I recommend you think first and foremost about what you want to play, and how you want to tackle your next adventure.

If you like the idea of being the only one in the group who understands, or at least tries to understand what is going on, you could play as a Professor. But if you prefer facing your problems head-on and laughing in the face of danger, you should choose the Daredevil.

If you see no problem in blowing up an ancient pillar to create a bridge in a handful of seconds, the Technician is who you're looking for.

Anyway, always remember that you should never have two Adventurers playing the same Role, unless it is unavoidable.

Other Roles

In the outgunned corebook you can find 10 Roles perfect for any occasion, like the Agent, the Nobody, and the Criminal.

When playing adventure, you are always free to use all the Roles and Tropes from outgunned and other expansions if you feel they would be appropriate.

Special Role

In addition to normal Roles, this genre book also includes a Special Role: the Fortune Seeker .

A Special Role simultaneously acts as your Role, your Trope, and your Background, and you should mark it down in all three sections on your Adventurer Sheet. This means that if you pick a Special Role, you can NOT choose a Trope.

Unlike other Roles, a Special Role immediately grants you 2 Attribute Points and 14 assigned Skill Points . Additionally, you gain 6 Skill Points to assign freely (instead of the usual 2), and can choose up to 3 Feats from the list offered by your Special Role.

The Fortune Seeker is the perfect Role to pick if you want to play an exceptional hero like the main character of a movie. I especially recommend this Special Role when you set off on an adventure with a small group or maybe even on your own.

Alternative Feats

When creating your

Adventurer, you can ask the Director

to substitute 1 of your Role Feats with another Feat taken from the Outgunned

corebook or from another expansion.

Similarly, if you pick a Role from another sourcebook, you can choose to swap out one of your Feats with another of your choice among the ones included in Outgunned Adventure.

Bear in mind that all Feats that require Adrenaline to be activated can also be activated using Luck, and vice versa.

TROPES

The Trope is the archetype of your Adventurer, and it allows you to get a quick look into their character and mannerisms. As in every good movie, some Adventurers are Wild at Heart and live in close contact with nature, and others are Salty Dogs with sea water running through their veins. Some Adventurers are Cowardly Lions who will try their best to hide, until they finally show everyone what they’re made of.

Once you’ve settled on a Role for your Adventurer, you must choose one of these 15 Tropes:

Action Archeologist Detestable Bastard Parental Figure
Adventuring Author Dreamer Proper Gentleman/Lady
Born Rebel Flying Steamroller Reluctant Hero
Cold and Distant Gentle Giant Salty Dog
Cowardly Lion Indispensable Rogue Wild at Heart

When you choose a Trope, you gain 1 point in an Attribute and 1 point each in 8 Skills . Then, you can choose 1 Feat from the list of 4 for the Trope. Additionally, each Trope gives you an interesting prompt about how to portray your Adventurer.

You’ll see that each Trope is connected to 2 Attributes. You're free to choose either of them when assigning your Attribute Point. For example, if you choose to play the Wild at Heart Trope, you gain 1 point in either Brawn or Nerves, your choice.

If your Role has already given you a point in one of the Attributes associated with your Trope, then you must choose the other one.

Archetypes, Not Stereotypes

Adhering to a Trope helps you dive straight into the action with a reasonably clear idea of how your Adventurer will act during the mission, and it also helps other players get an idea of who your Adventurer is and how to interact with them. But you must be careful not to take it too far.

When playing as your Adventurer you should always keep in mind their Trope, but never adhere to it blindly. Even Adventurers are well-rounded characters, and there’s no need for them to become stereotypes or robots, incapable of change!

Using Feats

Most Feats are always active, granting you Free Re-rolls when you are acting in a specific way, using a specific weapon or tool, or driving your favorite vehicle. Free Re-rolls greatly increase your chances of success, as such, it’s important that you pick your Feats carefully.

Some Feats have the symbol 4 beside their name. This means that they can only be activated by spending 1 Luck .

Finally, some Feats are preceded by the phrase [Quick Action] , and this means that you need to spend a Quick Action to activate them. Similarly, to activate a Feat preceded by the phrase [Full Turn] , you give up the chance to roll dice or take actions on that turn.

We’ll go in depth about Free Re-rolls, Luck, and actions later, in Section 2: BASICS OF THE GAME .

Rolling Dice

Dice are your best friends and your greatest enemies, they can spell the difference between a fun trip and a one-way journey. And if you want to be an Adventurer, you better get used to this, because the road to treasure is paved with dice and traps.

When playing adventure, you shouldn’t roll dice at every turn. Dice rolls are reserved for situations where there’s something at stake , you catch my drift? Outrunning a rolling boulder. Sneaking into a museum. Avoiding a volley of arrows. Deceiving your nemesis. Dodging bullets. Landing during a blizzard. These are the sort of things that require a good old dice roll.

If something can go wrong , roll the dice.

ACTiON & REACTiON

There are two types of rolls in this game:

Action Rolls : What you do is up to you. You’re free to choose what Attribute and Skill to call into play, describe your action, and roll the dice. Jason: The bridge has collapsed, so I guess I will have to jump across. I make an Action Roll in Brawn+Stunt.

Reaction Rolls : The Director tells you what is happening and how to react. The Attribute and Skill you must use to deal with this situation is up to them.

Director: As you’re clinging to the tree branch, a pesky monkey starts walking on your face. Make a Reaction Roll in Nerves+Endure in order to keep hanging on.

What’s at stake?

When facing a roll there is always something on the line. Having a clear image of what’s at stake allows the Director to determine what the consequences of a success or failure should be.

Broadly speaking, a nice success always means you reached your goal. You’ve done it once again!

A failure , on the other hand, means things didn’t really go as planned. You win some, you lose some.

Attributes and Skills

When you’re making a roll, you must always use one Attribute and one Skill. Although Skills are listed under the Attribute they are most often paired with, you can always use any Skill with any Attribute.

When shooting, you’ll often roll Nerves+Shoot, and when throwing punches, you’ll roll Brawn+Fight, but you can also use your imagination.

To hit a goon after sneaking up behind them, you can roll Crime+Fight. To find a way out while you’re driving, you can roll Nerves+Awareness. To make a good impression on someone with your sophisticated taste, you can roll Smooth+Know.

What could go wrong?

If you’re ever in doubt about which Attribute or Skill to use for a roll, ask yourself “What could go wrong?”

When you have a clear idea of the consequences of your failure, it will be easier to think about a way to avoid them.

Dice Pool

After choosing an Attribute and a Skill, add up the two scores . The total is the number of dice you must roll. To play adventure you can use the special Adventure Dice, made to allow you to spot successes easily, but you can also use common 6-sided dice.

When you are acting under favorable circumstances or make smart choices, the Director can also reward you with +1 to your roll. Similarly, when you act recklessly or the circumstances are against you, the Director can slap you with a -1.

All things considered, you can never roll less than 2 or more than 9 dice.

ROLL DiFFiCULTY

The higher the difficulty, the higher success you’ll need to pass the roll. There are 4 levels of difficulty:

  • ♦ BASIC: A quick and easy action or reaction. Like jumping over an obstacle, stealing the keys off of a distracted agent, throwing something.

  • ♦ CRITICAL: A full action or reaction. Like bursting through a sturdy door, jumping out of a window, earning someone’s trust.

  • ♦ EXTREME: A truly demanding action or reaction. Like solving a complex enigma, shooting a speeding target at night in the pouring rain, persuading a sworn enemy to cooperate.

  • ♦ IMPOSSIBLE: A desperate action or reaction. Like accurately hitting a target while you’re plummeting down from a burning seaplane, dealing with a whole squadron of soldiers with no weapons at all, taking down a fighter jet using nothing but an umbrella and a flock of seagulls. Epic stuff.

Double Difficulty

When you’re making an especially complex or dangerous roll, the Director can choose to set a double difficulty for it. This means that the roll will have difficulty equal to 2 Basic , 2 Critical , or 2 Extreme (but never 2 Impossible. Jeez!)

Rule of the Dice

You don’t need to roll dice for everything you do, but once the dice are rolled, there is no going back.

If you try to lift a car and score an Impossible Success, you’ve done it... don’t ask me how!

As such, the Director must do more than simply setting a difficulty for the roll. They must also and especially determine when and if you need to roll the dice.

When you face a roll with double difficulty, you still roll the dice only once, but you are looking to score 2 successes to prevail. And in the event of a failure, you’ll have to deal with 2 different consequences.

The only consolation is that, if you score at least one of the successes you need, you can avoid one of the consequences.

This rule is more commonly applied to Reaction Rolls to respond to multiple dangers (like several people shooting at you from all directions).

Some especially stubborn Enemies will also require rolls with double difficulty in order for you to hit them or to avoid their blows. We will talk more about this in Section 3: DANGERS & ENEMIES .

The Director can also choose to set a double difficulty for Action or Reaction Rolls they deem taxing, or as a way to manage complex situations.

For example, trying to climb a rock wall while a squad of soldiers is shooting at you could require a roll with double difficulty. The same goes for attempting an extremely long jump, or for trying to swindle someone who is already on the defensive about you.

Setting the Difficulty

The Director is tasked with setting the difficulty for each roll by considering the circumstances.

In most cases, a roll should have Critical difficulty, so as to present a believable challenge for the Adventurers. Whenever in doubt, a Director can always set the difficulty of the roll to Critical, without causing much trouble.

The Director should set a Basic difficulty for all rolls that concern really simple things but still hide a small chance for accidents. For example, when a group of several Adventurers is trying to sneak in or climb over a wall, and there’s a chance that one of them might make a wrong move.

When an Adventurer tries to do a simple action in a field outside of their expertise, the Director can also ask them to make a Basic roll as a way to put them to the test. For example, when an Adventurer with a single point in Force tries to kick in an old door that barely holds onto its hinges.

The same goes for 2 Basic rolls, which are only slightly harder than that.

Extreme difficulty is reserved for truly demanding rolls, where success is certainly not a given. The Director should be deliberate when choosing where they place Extreme rolls in order to avoid Adventurers facing insurmountable challenges.

2 Critical rolls are a happy medium between the Critical and Extreme difficulties. The Director can always rely on this double difficulty to give Adventurers a reasonably hard time.

Finally, Impossible difficulty should only come into play when Adventurers have gotten themselves in big trouble. Like when someone has decided to charge head-on toward an entire army of trained enemies. Nothing is impossible!

The Director can choose to suspend their judgment on the difficulty of a roll, waiting to see what successes the Adventurers score with their rolls. For example, let’s say two Adventurers are searching an apartment for weapons. One of them scores a Basic Success, the other, a Critical Success.

The Director determines that the Adventurer who scored a Basic Success only manages to break off a chair leg to use as a bat, while the Adventurer who scored a Critical Success is lucky enough to find a handgun in a drawer.

You can’t always get it right on the first try. If, after rolling the dice, you have scored at least one Basic Success, you can Re-roll .

Your new roll is considered better than the first if you roll an additional success or if you transformed one of your successes into a greater success.

Some Feats allow you to make Free Re-rolls in specific circumstances, or when you take specific actions. Help & Hindrance

When you're sinking in quicksand and can’t seem to find anything within reach, you can always rely on your trusty whip. However, even your most elegant clothes can become a hindrance when you need to leg it.

Over the course of your adventure, you will learn quickly that everything, even your friends, can be a Help or a Hindrance , heavily influencing the result of your actions.

Help

Some pieces of gear or Secondary Characters can grant you Help in specific circumstances. Your fellow Adventurers can also forgo rolling the dice to spend their turn trying to Help you face a difficult challenge. When you can rely on the Help of something or someone, the Director can grant you one of the following benefits, depending on the scope of the help:

  • ♦ Gain +1 to an Action or Reaction Roll. Director: Forcing this lock to open won’t be easy. Fortunately, you always carry your favorite set of lockpicks. You gain +1 to your Dexterity roll.

  • ♦ Gain a Success automatically, without rolling the dice. Director: Jason boosts you up and Alaska grabs your hand, hoisting you over the wall. Thanks to the Help of your friends, getting to the other side of the wall is easy as cake. You don’t even need to roll for it.

  • ♦ Gain the Requisite to make a roll. Director: Climbing this smooth wall of ice with your bare hands would be nearly impossible. Fortunately, you have a grappling hook, an essential Help that allows you to make this roll.

The Director should grant a +1 to the roll, when an Adventurer can rely on significant but not decisive Help. When the Help is great enough to nullify the risks and possibilities of failure, then it’s best to grant an automatic success .

Finally, when Help is instrumental to the action an Adventurer is attempting, the Director can allow the Adventurer to make a roll but grant no further bonus.

Whenever in doubt, if an Adventurer can rely on some Help, the Director should always grant a +1 to the roll and let the dice decide.

Grit is what makes you an Adventurer. When anyone else would run in panic, surrender, or give up hope, you just get back up and keep fighting to reach the treasure.

Grit is represented by a line of 12 boxes . Whenever you fail a Roll , you fill in a set number of boxes and stand back up after your failure.

A huge blade swinging from the ceiling? It misses your nose by an inch. The temple is crumbling around you? You jumped out at the last second and got off with a dusty jacket. Are they shooting at you? You just run, with bullets whistling in your ears. Nothing can stop you!

Even if you lose Grit, it doesn’t mean you become more compliant. Quite the opposite! Filling in Grit boxes only represents how many times your determination has managed to save your life.

Damage Control

When you make a Roll and fail to score the success you need, you can use any smaller successes to do Damage Control . You take some small desperate action to try to avoid losing at least some Grit. It’s better than doing nothing at all.

For every Basic Success you score, you can avoid losing 1 Grit, and for every Critical Success, you can avoid losing 3 Grit.

But when you make an Impossible Roll , you can never do Damage Control.

Director: They’re shooting at you! To dodge the bullets, react with Brawn+Stunt. It’s a Dangerous Critical Roll.

Jason: I roll the dice and score a Basic Success. I’ll use it to keep low while I run away.

You’re piloting a plane, one engine is failing AND you ended up in the middle of a blizzard. You’re plummeting down a waterfall AND getting shot at. When the situation gets especially dramatic or complex, the Director can determine that the Adventurer must make an Action or Reaction Roll with Multiple Difficulty .

You Look

When they tell you “You look tired” it’s probably because you practically have shopping bags under your eyes and you really need a good night’s sleep or a holiday. “You look hurt” means you’re losing a bit too much blood, or you’re dragging yourself around.

If “You look nervous”, I’m afraid your hands are shaking and you’re sweating cold, even more than you realized.

What I mean is that Adventurers are tough, they do their best to hide their weakness, either out of pride, or because they're protecting others, or because they have no time to think about themselves when all they can think about is the Treasure.

Unfortunately, there comes a time, when even Adventurers start feeling the physical and psycho-emotional blows of their adventure.

When you suffer small wounds or a reasonable stress, your Grit is enough to push forward and ignore the pain. But when you begin showing signs of trauma, when you look Hurt, Nervous, Scared, Distracted, or when you simply look Like a Fool, it’s time for Conditions to come into play.

CONDiTiONS

Conditions represent your physical, mental, or emotional status. They tell you how you're doing, feeling, and how you look to others.

On your Sheet, you find the 5 core Conditions, each tied to an Attribute. When you get one of these Conditions, you suffer -1 to all rolls made with that Attribute until you find a way to treat it.

When you already have 3 Conditions and you should suffer a fourth, you instead get the Broken Condition, which inflicts an additional -1 to all rolls. Once you look Broken, you cannot receive more Conditions.

On your Sheet, you will also find the Tired Condition, which gives no Penalty, but brings you one step closer to looking Broken, as well as some blank lines to note other Conditions that are either less common or created by your Director.

End of an Adventurer

Even Grit has its limits, and if they don’t play their hand carefully, even the greatest Adventurer runs the risk of kicking the bucket.

When you have lost all Grit, you’re in big trouble. Your next failure in a Roll will force you to take a spin on the Death Roulette. Sounds cool, right?

Death Roulette

Next to your Conditions, there is a 6-bullet cylinder that represents the Death Roulette. The more this cylinder fills up with Lethal Bullets , the closer you are to your doom.

When you’re about to be Left for Dead, the Director will let you take a spin on the Death Roulette , and this single die roll will decide whether you survive or you have finally rolled your last. That’s quite the stakes, right?

When the Director asks you to take a spin on the Death Roulette, roll a numeric six-sided die.

If the result is greater than the number of Lethal Bullets in your roulette, you just had a brush with death. Add a Lethal Bullet to your cylinder, describe how you managed to cheat death once again, and keep on playing.

If the result is equal to the number of Lethal Bullets in your roulette or lower, I’m afraid to say that you’ll be Left for Dead ... unless a friend saves you at the last second by using one of their Spotlights .

Remember, you begin the game with 1 Lethal Bullet in the cylinder, and you add one more whenever you have a narrow escape. You see where I’m going with this?

Thanks to the Death Roulette, you always have a chance to escape from your doom, but this will become increasingly more difficult.

Even a cat will run out of lives sooner or later. There is no way to remove a Lethal Bullet from your Death Roulette. The good news is that the roulette goes back down to 1 at the beginning of a new Cinematic campaign .

Saving a Friend

Even if an Adventurer has failed their roll for the Death Roulette, all is not lost. Another Adventurer who has a Spotlight can choose to spend it to intervene at the last second and miraculously save their friend from certain death.

When this happens, the saved Adventurer must add a Lethal Bullet to their cylinder while the Adventurer who used the spotlight is free to describe the epic actions or cunning plan that led to a happy resolution.

Left for Dead

If an Adventurer fails their roll for the Death Roulette and none of the others can intervene to save them, the Adventurer is Left for Dead.

As the term suggests, an Adventurer who is Left for Dead is not necessarily dead. It’s more like taking a dramatic bow and leaving everyone heartbroken.

When you’re Left for Dead, it means you have fallen out of an airplane or down a cliff, you have plunged into the ocean, were buried by rubble, or suffered a wound that looked lethal, and your friends were forced to leave you behind or run away. What I’m getting at is something happens and everyone is convinced that you couldn’t possibly survive, but your body is never found.

In some cases, a Player might decide that their Adventurer is truly, unmistakably dead, but only if they feel like it would be climactic, interesting, or moving for the story. Honor to the fallen.

Back from the Dead

An Adventurer who is Left for Dead always has a chance to return suddenly with a preposterous story about how they managed to survive, get better, and find their way back to the group. When this happens, however, they always get a Scar from the circumstances of their departure.

The Director and Player are tasked with finding the best moment and way for the fallen Adventurer to return.

The best moments for an Adventurer to return on the scene are during a Point of No Return or a Moment of Truth. We will talk more about these special Shots in Section 7: TO ADVENTURE!.

Enemy Grit

The most important stat for an Enemy is their Grit, which is represented by a string of 3 to 12 boxes. The Director is free to choose how much Grit each Enemy has. A higher number of Grit boxes will cause the fight to last longer.

As a general rule, Enemies with Basic or Critical Defense should have between 3 and 6 Grit boxes. The Director can add boxes, up to a total of 12, when the Enemy is especially strong, or has great numbers.

Enemies with Extreme Defense usually have 3 Grit, and should never have more than 6 Grit boxes.

When the Adventurers land a hit on the Enemy, the Director fills in a Grit box, and when all boxes are full, the Enemy is defeated or forced to flee.

Remember that an Enemy could represent a single tough opponent, or a large number of ugly mugs. You should adapt the way you narrate lost Grit depending on the description of the Enemy.

If the Enemy is indeed an individual, lost Grit could represent bruises, wounds, or hard blows. But if the Enemy is a group of soldiers, each instance of lost Grit could represent one soldier getting knocked out.

Enemy Defense

The Defense score of an Enemy represents the difficulty of Action Rolls that Adventurers need to land blows. Whenever you score a success equal to the Defense, the Enemy loses 1 Grit. If you score a higher success, the Enemy loses 3 Grit.

For example, if you’re attacking an Enemy with Critical Defense and score a Critical Success, the Enemy loses 1 Grit. If you score an Extreme Success, the Enemy loses 3 Grit. If you score a Basic Success, you cannot land a blow on the Enemy.

The Director can set the Enemy Defense as:

  • ♦ Basic: The Enemy is very easy to hit or wound. This is ideal for low-stakes combat scenes, or situations where weaker Adventurers are fighting alone.

  • ♦ Critical: The Enemy is a tough nut, and wounding them is no easy feat. Most Enemies worthy of the name have Critical Defense.

  • ♦ Extreme: The Enemy’s Defense is almost perfect. It will be extremely difficult for Adventurers to land blows, let alone defeat them.

Enemy Attack

When the Enemy attacks, all Adventurers involved in combat will have to make a Reaction Roll with difficulty equal to the Enemy Attack.

For example, if an Enemy has Critical Attack, it means Adventurers will need to score a Critical Success to dodge or parry their strikes. Failing to do so will result in the Adventurers losing Grit, exactly as would happen with any other Dangerous Rolls.

The Director can set the Enemy Attack as:

  • ♦ Basic: The Enemy is really nothing impressive, their blows miss the mark often.

  • ♦ 2 Basic: The Enemy is a normal thug, or an untrained or ill-equipped individual.

  • ♦ Critical: The Enemy knows what they're doing, their blows can do some damage. Most Enemies worthy of the name have Critical Attack.

  • ♦ 2 Critical: The Enemy is very strong, and it’s likely that their blows will do at least some damage. The Adventurers will often have to settle for some Damage Control.

  • ♦ Extreme: The Enemy has superhuman strength or lethal weapons. Withstanding their attacks is a heroic feat.

When the Enemy is in a tight spot or in a disadvantageous position, the Director could also choose to lower their Attack score by 1 or 2 levels for a Turn.

Facing an Enemy i ee

When an Enemy steps onto the scene, combat begins.

Combat doesn’t need to be a clash to the death between two opposing sides. Any Scene where an Enemy which you can possibly defeat is trying to get rid of you can be considered a Combat scene.

During combat, there are two alternating turns:

Action Turn : Adventurers are free to take any action they want and make the relative Action Rolls. Additionally, all Adventurers have the right to a free Quick Action, which they can use to reload a gun, get behind partial cover, or take an item from their bag.

Alaska: I shoot blindly toward the soldiers and dive behind partial cover.

Reaction Turn : The Director states the Enemies’ actions and all Adventurers must defend themselves with a Reaction Roll ft .

Director: The soldiers open fire with their machine guns. Everyone, roll Brawn+Stunt to duck out of the way.

During combat, you are not required to attack the Enemy and can choose to take any other action or even run away. But until you manage to get rid of them, the Enemy will keep causing Reaction Rolls a and put your Grit to the test!

initiative

There is only one rule: the first to strike is the first to strike!

If the Adventurers choose to attack the Enemy, combat begins with an Action Turn.

On the other hand, if the Director is the first to state that the Enemy attacks the Adventurers, or if the Adventurers hesitate or get caught by surprise, then combat begins with a Reaction Turn.

When in doubt, the Director can flip a coin to decide.

Attacking first always gives you an advantage, so you must be ready to swing first when the situation really requires a fight. On the other hand, you shouldn’t always default to violence when there are other options. The right balance is somewhere in between.

Action Turn during combat

During an Action Turn every Adventurer can make a Quick Action for free as well as an Action Roll.

An Adventurer can choose whether to use their Quick Action before or after making their Action Roll.

When taking their Quick Action , the Adventurer can grab or toss an object, reload a weapon, reach an Enemy, or take another quick and easy action. See Extra Actions in Section 2: BASICS OF THE GAME .

When making their Action Roll , an Adventurer can attempt any kind of complex action, including attacking the Enemy.

To attack an Enemy, the Adventurer must make a roll with difficulty equal to the Enemy Defense . For every appropriate success scored, the Enemy loses 1 Grit.

As such, if you’re attacking an Enemy with Basic Defense and score 1 Basic Success, the Enemy loses 1 Grit, and if you score 2 Basic Successes, the Enemy loses 2 Grit.

Similarly, if you attack an Enemy with Critical Defense and score 1 or 2 Critical Successes, the Enemy loses 1 or 2 Grit, but if you only score a Basic Success, you can’t land a blow.

Usually, when attacking an Enemy in melee, you roll Brawn+Fight, but feel free to get creative with your actions. There is no limit to the Skills and Attributes you can call into play to give your opponent a hard time.

For example, you can roll Nerves+Drive to ram your car into your enemies, or roll Focus+Fix to knock some scaffolding down over their heads. Any action that might hurt, tire, discourage, or chase away your enemies will result in them losing some Grit.

As a general rule, all actions that directly involve the Enemy will have difficulty equal to their Defense, while all collateral actions can have a different difficulty chosen by the Director, much like what happens out of combat.

Reaction Turn during combat

During a Reaction Turn, the Director describes the action of the Enemy and asks all Adventurer involved in combat to make a Reaction Roll .

This means that all Reaction Rolls made during combat are Dangerous Rolls. Seems obvious.

In combat the difficulty of Reaction Rolls is equal to the Enemy Attack .

As such, if you are fighting against an Enemy with Basic Attack, you must make a Basic Reaction Roll or risk losing 1 Grit. If the Enemy has Critical Attack, you must make a Critical Reaction Roll and run the risk of losing 3 Grit.

As usual, you can spend any smaller success rolled during combat to do Damage Control .

If you have any doubt on the subject of Rolls or losing Grit, run back to the chapter on Dangerous Rolls in Section 3: DANGERS & ENEMIES .

If you don’t know what roll to ask the Adventurers to make, here is a handy list of examples. Feel free to come up with your own Reaction Rolls, choose from this list, or even roll at the beginning of the turn to randomize the Turn.

1. The enemies attack you with fists and bats. Roll Brawn+Fight

2. The enemies shoot at you. Roll Brawn+Stunt

3. The enemies try to grapple or choke you. Roll Brawn+Endure

4. The enemies try to push you the ground. Roll Brawn+Force

5. The enemies throw tables and chairs at you. Roll Nerves+Stealth

6. The enemies try to surround you. Roll Nerves+Awareness

DOES EVERYBODY REACT THE SAME WAY?

Usually, all Adventurers fighting against the same Enemy defend themselves by making the same Reaction Roll with the same Attribute and Skill.

Why? Because...

  • ♦ It’s easy. Asking all Adventurers to make the same roll allows you to easily manage any kind of situation. When you first begin directing sessions, you could find it pretty hard to come up with interesting Reaction Rolls , to choose the right Skill to test, and to manage failures in creative ways. So, having to do this only once for all Adventurers is probably best, don’t you think?

  • ♦ It’s fast. “Everyone roll Brawn+Stunt!” See? That’s quick. Sometimes combat can drag on for several Turns, and while everyone loves a good scuffle, it’s important that you find strategies to keep everyone on their toes and avoid slowing down the action.

  • ♦ It’s fair. If EVERYONE makes the same roll, there are no favorites. This time, one Adventurer rolls 3 dice and another rolls 6, but maybe the next time the tables will turn. It’s extremely important to keep a sense of fairness and equity for everyone at the table, as this helps Players remain honest.

That being said, there may be complex situations when the Adventurers are scattered all over, and then the Director can choose to ask for a different Reaction Roll from each Adventurer.

This strategy can create great tension, and bring to light complex and exciting dynamics, but it runs the risk of extending a combat scene, which means it should be used sparingly and managed carefully.

Assist

You scored a lower success than needed and don’t really know what to do with it? You now have a way to donate it to another Adventurer in need. No more wasted successes!

The Assist is a new tool that allows you to share some of your successes with others, and it can prove useful during scenes when the Adventurers are all acting together to further a shared goal, for example during a fight scene, or a chase .

Making an assist

When you make an Action Roll and score a lower success that doesn’t allow you to overcome the challenge at hand, you can choose to turn it into an Assist for your friends. In practical terms, making an Assist means leaving a success on the table, so that another Adventurer may take advantage of it to improve their next roll.

When making an Assist, you must describe as much as possible which actions you are taking to create an opportunity for your allies.

For example, you can try to distract an Enemy, get them to chase you, or you could try to create a diversion, or even hold out your hand to a friend so that they may grab onto it.

Raymond: I try to attack the shady characters. I roll the dice and score a pretty Basic Success.

Raymond: Cripes! Well, since my blows don’t seem enough to hurt them, I want to use this Basic Success to make an Assist. I throw my bag at one of the brutes, hoping he will reflexively grab for it.

When you score an extra success lower than what you’d need to do additional damage, the Director could also allow you to spend it to make an Assist.

Using an assist

When a success on the table is what you need to tip the scales of your roll, you can use the Assist from another Adventurer to improve your result.

For example, if you need an Extreme Success and scored 2 Critical Successes, the Assist from a friend could grant you that third Critical Success you needed to make the roll.

When this happens, you also need to describe how your actions interact with those of your companion in order to use the Assist and achieve your success.

Alaska: I see Raymond is in trouble and run to clobber the brutes with him. I roll the dice and score 2 Basic Successes. That’s still not enough, but I can use Raymond’s Assist! I combine his Basic Success to mine and get a Critical Success. One of the shady dudes grabs the bag and since his hands are now occupied, I get a chance to punch him in the face.

You can only use the Assist from another Adventurer if they have made their Action Roll before you and only until the end of the Action Turn. You cannot make or use Assists during Reaction Turns.

What does an Assist do?

The Assist has 3 main goals:

  • ♦ Encouraging team play and the shared narrative. Thanks to this tool, the Adventurers have a chance to mechanically interact with each other, and they are motivated to pay attention to the actions of their companions.

  • ♦ Giving weight to smaller or extra successes. Sometimes, Adventurers score lower successes or extra successes and see no creative way to make use of them. The Assist can be the perfect prompt on how to turn these small successes into great opportunities.

  • ♦ Adding a pinch of strategy. To get the best out of the Assist, the Adventurers must choose carefully who will roll first. Should the most prepared of them go first and leave their potential extra successes for the others; or should the Adventurer with the smaller dice pool go first, since they may not reach the target success? A pinch of strategy never hurts.

Extended Rolls

Chases, sports competitions, group stealth, exploration, attempts at building or repairing a ride... There are many challenges that an Adventurer can’t hope to carry out with a simple roll of the dice. For any such instances, you have Extended Rolls!

Extended Rolls cover activities that last for more than a single Action or Reaction Roll, and they can either involve a single Adventurer, or the whole group.

Usually, successfully passing in an Extended Roll can resolve a short scene or grant you huge advantages. Similarly, failing an Extended Roll is worse than getting a simple Action Roll wrong, and it carries consequences that will have a bigger impact on the story.

The Director can call for an Extended Roll when they wish to draw out a moment or grant more gravitas to an action.

Opposed Rolls

As the burning plane crashes against the side of the mountain, you and your Rival go plummeting through the air, gripping onto the same parachute. Who will manage to get it on?

Your traveling companion grabs onto the wheel and tries to change course, but you are determined to head straight for the waterfall that keeps getting closer. Who will prevail?

You grab one of the glasses in front of you and down it in one breath, begging your lucky stars that this is not the poisoned one. What will happen?

All these nail-biting moments filled with conflict and uncertainty can be resolved with an Opposed Roll .

In an Opposed Roll, two Players simultaneously pick up the dice to determine which of them will prevail.

This is also the only circumstance when a Director can roll the dice like Adventurers usually do. There’s no end to the surprises!

Types of Opposed Rolls

There are 3 types of Opposed Rolls:

Adventurer VS Director: When the Adventurer is facing off directly against an opponent, or standing against a force of nature, the Director can call for an Opposed Roll to determine the outcome of their efforts.

This type of challenge is definitely the most common Opposed Roll, and it’s designed to give more weight to a pivotal moment in the story. As a Director, you should opt for this type of roll when you want to extend a moment, giving the impression of things happening in slow-motion.

When making an Opposed Roll, the Director can roll any number of dice they choose, usually 5, 6, or sometimes even 7, depending on the opponent’s strength and the circumstances. If you feel that 5 dice are too many, perhaps the moment is not dramatic enough to require an Opposed Roll.

Director: June and the evil Colonel both dash toward the Treasure, but it’s impossible to know who will get there first. It’s the perfect context for an Opposed Roll.

MAKiNG AN OPPOSED ROLL

The two Players involved in an Opposed Roll are known as Contenders .

This is what you need to do, in order to make an Opposed Roll and best the other Contender:

  • ♦ Roll: You and the other Contender roll your pool of dice. When making an Opposed Roll you can only spend 1 Luck per Turn and can NOT use a Spotlight to succeed.

  • ♦ Contest: You and the other Contender examine your results, and determine who scored worse. For example, if you rolled a Critical + Basic Success and the other Contender only scored a Critical Success, they are the one with the worst result.

  • ♦ Re-roll and All In: The Contender with the worst result must Re-roll first, if they can. If their Re-roll manages to turn the tables, the other Contender also has a right to Re-roll to get an even better result. Then the same for going All In.

As usual, Adventurers who can rely on an appropriate Feat might make a Free Re-roll, but the Director can never make Free Re-rolls, unless they are rolling for Fortune (see Adventurer VS Fortune above).

  • ♦ Outcome: Whoever gets the best combination has won the challenge. The other Contender has lost, and the Director can even underline this burning defeat by assigning a relevant Condition.

  • If, even after Re-rolling and going All In, the Contenders are evenly matched, the outcome of the Opposed Roll is entrusted to a single, unappealable, coin flip.

You can lose gear as a consequence of your failure, or when you choose to sacrifice it to gain +1 to a roll that you think is extremely important.

Bag and Backpack

Rope, grappling hook, machete, winter clothes, hunting rifle, ammo, first-aid kit, and revolver… When you see your gear laid out on a table, you realize that you might not be traveling as light as you thought. Fortunately you can stick everything into your bag, or in the backpack!

Whenever you obtain a weapon or a piece of gear, the first thing you should decide is where you want to store it.

On You: All the items you keep in a pocket, in a holster, on your shoulder, or simply on your person should be noted in the main box of the Weapons & Gear section on your Sheet.

You can only carry up to 3 weapons on you, and only one of those can have the Bulky feat.

Bag: The most classic example of this is an old leather satchel that has seen better days, but all sacks, purses, travel trunks, and last but not least, briefcases can fall in this category.

Bags can only hold items of smaller size.

During combat scenes or flustered moments, getting an item out of or into your bag requires a Quick Action.

Backpack: All bulky containers you can carry on your back are considered backpacks.

Your backpack can hold all kinds of items as well as up to 3 additional weapons (only one of those can have the Bulky feat).

An empty backpack is no big deal, but a full backpack can always become a Hindrance on your Adventure, if the Director feels like it would be. During combat scenes or flustered moments, getting an item out of or into your backpack requires a Full Turn or an extra Full Action.

At the beginning of the game, you can choose whether to have a bag, a backpack, or even both if you’d prefer.

Over the course of your Adventure, you can usually get a new bag or backpack and, in most cases, you don’t need to spend Cash to acquire such things. However, you can never carry more than one bag and one backpack at the same time without them becoming a severe Hindrance.

The benefits of having a bag or backpack

The main advantage of carrying several items in a bag is that you can take them off, pick them back up, or hand them over to a friend as a single gesture.

The Key

An ancient artifact that can reveal the location of the Treasure, a map nobody can decipher, a mysterious key, or a diary stuffed with clues, these are all perfect examples of Key Items .

A Key Item, also simply called a Key , is a crucial tool you need in order to find, reach, or identify the Treasure. Without this item, you would never be able to reach the goal of your adventure.

The distinctive trait of a Key Item is that you can never lose it just because you failed a roll . Whenever you run the risk of losing the Key, you lose 1 Grit instead , as a way to represent the small heart attack you get when you think you’ve lost such an instrumental object.

Instead of being lost, the Key is still safe in your hands, or rolling on the floor to a spot where you can see it and retrieve it with a bit of effort.

If you are Out of Grit when you run the risk of losing the Key, you instead suffer a Condition chosen by the Director.

For example, if you fail a roll and drop your bag, where you were keeping the Key, you automatically lose 1 Grit and have a chance to describe how you manage to keep a hold of the Key. Perhaps there was a hole in your bag and the Key fell to the floor, or you suddenly remember that you had moved it to a pocket in your jacket and can breathe easy once again.

Getting the Key

You can obtain a Key Item:

  • ♦ At the beginning of the Adventure, because you inherited it, or because someone hands it to you as they send you off to look for the Treasure.

  • ♦ In a Temple filled with traps of all kinds.

  • ♦ From the Rival , provided that you manage to outsmart, defeat, or rob them.

Usually there is only one Key Item in each adventure, and the Adventurer who carries it needs to make it clear to everyone at the table by placing a coin, a key, a special die, or another marker in front of their Sheet.

While at Camp, you are free to pass the Key to any of your companions without issue. Outside of Camp, handing over the Key to someone else always causes you to lose 1 Grit, to represent how crucial that act is.

Cash

There are things that money can’t buy, like a Treasure, or the dumbstruck expression on your Rival’s face when they realize you've played them once again. For everything else, there's Cash ($) !

What Cash is

Cash represents the money that you carry around, or that you have easy access to. Each $ doesn’t represent a specific sum in dollars, it’s a generic unit that represents your buying power. Banknotes in your wallet, watches you can pawn, and loose change can all be represented by your Cash.

You can have up to 5$, and you can spend it to buy gear, information, or other useful services.

What Cash is NOT

Cash is not an indicator of your general wealth or social status, but simply a resource you can spend to pay for goods and services while on your adventure.

Anyway, your Cash doesn’t tell you if you’re rich or not. It simply tells you which benefits you can get by spending the money or pawning the valuables you carry around.

If you have 1$, you’re not necessarily down on your luck. Maybe you really are poor as a church-mouse, or maybe you’re a wealthy heir who suddenly finds themselves short on cash and who can’t wait to get back to their huge mansion. I wonder how the butler is doing.

Earning Cash

All Adventurers start the game with 1$ in their pocket, and they can decide to spend it immediately to buy some additional gear. Some Roles and Tropes grant you the Moneybags Feat, which grants you additional Cash that you either earned or inherited.

During your adventure, you can get additional Cash by selling some of your possessions, or simply by borrowing it from your beloved enemies.

Anyway, it’s hard for an Adventurer to get their hands on more than 1$ at a time, unless they get their hands on a safe or some such thing. And when you re-sell an item, most buyers will only be willing to pay one third of the initial price. For example, it takes 1$ to buy a revolver, but to earn that same 1$ you’ll need to sell 3 revolvers at once. And the buyer will insist they are doing you a favor!

The Temple

When you lay eyes on it, you feel your heart in your throat. Imposing, magnificent, beautiful… but also surrounded by an aura of danger and death that makes the hair on your nape stand up when you grab the torch and venture in.

It has been centuries since anyone has set foot in this temple, and you are well aware that the next step could be your last.

In adventure, a temple is any abandoned, hidden, or forgotten structure that is by nature or design nearly impossible to locate or reach. Every temple hides a secret in the maze of its halls, a Key or a Treasure, which are always protected by deadly traps , enigmas, and dangers of all kinds.

A pyramid that lies buried underneath the Sahara desert, an abandoned castle in the Black Forest, a Buddhist temple at the heart of Nepal, or the ancient catacombs that sprawl beneath the streets of Paris are all perfect examples of temples. The temple marks the moment when time slows down, when the breakneck pace of the action finally leaves room for cautious, tense exploration. Here, the Adventurers are forced to evaluate very carefully their every move, and called on to prove their knowledge and skills in order to reach their goal. Only the most prepared, or the luckiest, will reemerge to see the light of day.

Who built the temple?

When you set the scene for a nice temple, the first thing you should ask yourself is: who built this? And why? After all, if someone went to the trouble of hiding something from the whole World, they must have had a very good reason!

Kinds of Traps

You open the door and trigger a trap. You pull the lever and trigger a trap. You grab the treasure and trigger a trap. You get in and trigger a trap. You don’t… and still trigger a trap!

Run outline

A run scene is not a competition and it is not your everyday chase, it is a desperate race for survival.

When the Adventurers are faced with mortal danger, and standing still could spell certain doom, that’s when they need to run.

A run scene consists of several consecutive Reaction Turns where all rolls are Dangerous Rolls.

Run duration

Run scenes last for a predetermined number of Turns, or until the Adventurers find a way to get out of it.

Usually Adventurers need to run for 3 or 5 consecutive Turns , depending on how complex the scene is. The Director is free to choose the duration they deem fitting, and they are not required to disclose it to the Adventurers.

I recommend that the Director mark down a number of boxes equal to the run duration. Whenever the Adventurers face a Reaction Turn, the Director can fill in one box.

Winning a run

If the Adventurers manage to survive until the end of the run, they can claim to be victorious.

Alternatively, the Adventurers can try to leave the run scene ahead of time, for example, by finding a way to stop the source of their troubles, or by finding a safe place to hide.

Losing a run

If all Adventurers lose all Grit before the end of a run scene, they have lost. Nobody could survive that, but the Adventurers are always lucky enough to find a way out even from this kind of situation.

When they lose a run, the Adventurers are usually captured by the enemy, or end up trapped in the rubble, or sealed in a crypt with no way out in sight.

During a Cinematic Campaign, losing a Run could also cause the Heat to rise.

Truth or Superstition?

Before you set off on your next adventure, ask yourself this question: is the supernatural real?

  • ♦ Yes, but it doesn’t appear to be: The Adventurers have never made contact with anything supernatural before, and they will need tangible proof before accepting the kind of trouble they are now in. If you choose this option, you need to ration mysterious events and plausible explanations carefully, always leaving room for doubt. Only in the very end will the supernatural reveal itself in its full, unfathomable force.

  • Cinematic Examples: Raiders of the Lost Ark, the Last Crusade

  • ♦ No, but it appears to be: There are many events with no apparent explanation, and at one point, the Adventurers come to believe there must be an otherworldly force at play. The truth is that there is actually a logical (sort of) and scientific explanation to everything, but they will only discover this at the very end. The undead army they had to fight? Gravely ill soldiers. The legendary guardians? Obtained their strength thanks to a medicinal plant. Cinematic Examples: Uncharted (videogame), Tomb Raider

  • ♦ Yes, and it appears to be: The supernatural elements are clear from the very beginning, leaving no room for doubt. The Adventurers know what they are dealing with, and they are too busy fighting mythical beasts and Egyptian plagues to ask questions.

  • Cinematic Examples: The Mummy (1999), Jumanji

  • ♦ No, and it doesn’t appear to be: Those are only legends and superstitions. I know it, you know it, the Adventurers know it, and nobody ever doubts this. No harm in a little fiction, but let’s keep it within reason. Cinematic Examples: National Treasure, The Lost City

  • ♦ Nobody knows: You have yet to decide, and that’s just fine. Add a mysterious element or a suspicious clue here and there, and watch for the Adventurers’ reaction. Was that a yeti or just a huge guy in furs? Who knows! You can wait until the very end to decide if there’s any truth to the legends.

Cinematic Campaign

An adventure Cinematic Campaign is usually composed of 5-8 Shots, and follows the Adventurers in their search for the Treasure to prevent it from being taken by the Rival.

A Cinematic Campaign always begins with a Pilot Shot that lays down the foundations of the story, and it always ends with a moving Moment of Truth where everything comes together and the true nature of the Treasure is revealed.

At the very middle of a Cinematic Campaign, there is also a special Shot called the Point of No Return , which represents a milestone in the adventure, and marks the Adventurers’ Advancement .

Pilot Shot

As the name implies, the Pilot Shot is what steers the Adventurers onto the path to the Treasure, starting off the adventure. A good Pilot Shot should give the Adventurers some room to get in the game and get to know their companions, but it should also clearly introduce the theme and tone of the Cinematic Campaign.

Here are 3 classic Pilot Shots that can help you start your adventure off on the right foot:

  • ♦ The Expedition: This Shot begins with all Adventurers gathered in a safe place, like in the home of an old friend or sponsor, or in an old-timey pub, or in the office of their new boss. The person who gathered them all there wants to find the Treasure, and they might even have a Key to point them in the right direction.

This Pilot Shot is perfect for when you need to bring together Adventurers who don’t know each other, give them important intel on the Treasure, and gently nudge the adventure to start.

  • ♦ The MacGuffin: The Adventurers are in the middle of a wild location or in a temple full of traps, and they are looking for an artifact that probably has nothing in common with their impending adventure. After many challenges, the Adventurers come face to face and probably get steamrolled by the Rival, or get their hands on an important clue that steers them toward the real Treasure.

  • This Pilot Shot uses a “fake Treasure” as a stratagem to throw the Adventurers straight into the middle of the action.

  • ♦ The Accident: The Adventurers are on a journey, they are working at an archeological dig, or are in the middle of a job for an old client. What started like a normal day for them is soon turned upside-down by an accident that changes their lives forever, like the discovery of a secret entrance, a terrible shipwreck on a desert island, or an ambush by the Rival’s goons. And this is how the Adventurers learn about the Treasure.

This Pilot Shot is more unpredictable than the others, but when managed well it can prove thrilling and leave ample room for the Adventurers to prove what they are made of from the very beginning.

Point of No Return

The Point of No Return is a special Shot that leaves the Adventurers either physically or metaphorically with no way back, and gives them the determination to give their all for the Treasure.

Most likely, during a Point of No Return the Adventurers will begin setting their own personal motives aside as they discover or understand that stopping the Rival and preventing them from reaching the Treasure is the only thing that matters. Everything else can wait.

Here are 4 classic moments that mark the perfect Point of No Return:

  • ♦ Faraway from Civilization: The Adventurers are lost in the unexplored wilderness and will have no chance to leave it before the end of the Campaign.

  • ♦ Now it’s Personal: The Rival has captured someone or done something terrible and unforgivable. They must be stopped at all costs.

  • ♦ Trapped: The Adventurers are trapped or locked into a tomb or temple. The only thing that matters is getting out of there.

  • ♦ Supernatural Fury: A supernatural creature made its existence known to the whole World, or at least to the whole city. The World is forever changed.

In shorter Campaigns, you usually have only one Point of No Return, located at the mid-way point between the Pilot Shot and the Moment of Truth. Longer Campaigns can also sport 2 Points of No Return, if needed.

After a Point of No Return, everything is different and the Adventurers are completely entangled in a race against the Rival for the Treasure, with no time to rest. From then on, every long-term plan or call for help from the authorities is destined to fail.

The Point of No Return marks the moment when all Adventurers Advance , gaining new Feats and Skill Points to represent their personal growth.

The Moment of Truth

The Moment of Truth is the final Shot that marks the end of a Cinematic Campaign, and it can usually be played out in 1 or 2 Sessions, depending on how complex it is.

During the Moment of Truth, the Adventurers will have to make difficult choices and learn what is truly important in their eyes. In this Shot, the Treasure finally reveals its true nature, whatever it may be, and (almost!) all secrets are revealed.

Additionally, during a perfect Moment of Truth, the Rival will meet their bitter end and be forced to pay dearly for their dastardly deeds.

Regardless of the secrets revealed during a Moment of Truth, one thing is certain: at the end of the Campaign, the Adventurers never walk home with the Treasure. Whether it is due to a twist of fate, because it poses a great danger, or simply because it belongs there, in its ancient resting place, the Treasure is not made to be taken, and the Moment of Truth always shows why.

At the beginning of the Moment of Truth, the Adventurers can make their final Advancement .

From this moment, the Heat stops rising, and the Adventurers who use a Spotlight cannot flip a coin in order to keep it. The stakes are sky-high!

The Rival’s Desire

Facing the Rival

So Many Rivals!

Usually, the Rival is a single individual who can rely on the means necessary to make life difficult for an entire group of Adventurers.

But some adventures could star two different Rivals who split the Strong Spots among them and cooperate more or less willingly in an attempt to reach the Treasure.

Finally, there is always a chance that the first Rival the Adventurers meet will turn out to be nothing but a henchman, a red herring, and that they will be replaced by the true Rival, who can always rely on one more Strong Spot than the first.

The Rival is more than a simple opponent, they are an extremely dangerous individual, and will stop at nothing in order to get their hands on the Treasure.

This is why, when you face a Rival, you must keep a few things in mind:

  • ♦ The Rival can only lose during the Moment of Truth and can’t be defeated in earlier Shots. A Rival can also meet their bitter end during a Point of No Return, only to get replaced by another, stronger and eviler than them.

  • ♦ The Rival is a Hindrance up until the Moment of Truth. Whenever the Adventurers are interacting with the Rival, they always roll with a Hindrance due to their cunning, strength, or their goons. The Rival stops being a Hindrance at the beginning of the Moment of Truth.

  • ♦ You can't spend a Spotlight to hurt, capture, or swindle the Rival , unless you are playing the Moment of Truth.

  • ♦ The Rival is cunning , which means they are always one step ahead. Even if the Adventurers manage to outrun the Rival and destroy every clue in their wake, their nemesis will always find a way to remain hot on their trail.

  • ♦ The Rival is also stupid , which means they are never more than one step ahead. Even if the Adventurers are defeated and thrown off the scent repeatedly, the Rival will never manage to get much further without their intervention. It doesn’t take much for real Adventurers to turn the tables.

Heat

As you get closer to the Treasure, you can feel the Rival breathing down your neck. Every step you take, every trap you overcome, every enemy you face brings you closer to the goal of a lifetime, and when the stakes are this high, the heat is bound to skyrocket. No wonder if all Adventurers are always covered in a light sheen of sweat!

Heat is a dangerous clock that goes from 1 to 12, it tracks whether the Adventurers are still in the lead or if their Rival has managed to get an advantage over them.

This tool is designed for Cinematic Campaigns, and as such holds a place of honor on the Travel Journal.

Whenever the Adventurers fail to reach an objective, get delayed, or when the Rival gets the upper hand, the Heat rises and the Director earns some dangerous Heat Points , which they can use to raise the stakes and make things even more complicated.

What does Heat do?

Now and then the Adventurers fail to reach a crucial objective, they get captured, they lose a fight, they don’t manage to win a race, or they simply dawdle more than they should, allowing the Rival to act freely. Even so, in adventure, failure doesn’t exist.

Heat is a tool that the Director can use to give weight and very real consequences to big mistakes made by the Adventurers, without causing them to fail ruinously.

Did the Adventurers fail the run? The rolling boulder behind them destroys everything in their path and they end up bruised in a pile of rubble, but still alive: Heat rises by 1. Are the Adventurers lost in the jungle and unable to find their way? They wander for hours before finding a landmark, and when they finally reach their destination night has fallen: Heat rises by 1.

Whenever the Director needs to give weight to one of the Adventurers’ mistake without leading them to a disastrous defeat, they can choose to raise the Heat.

Rising Heat

When the adventure begins, the Heat level is equal to the number of Adventurers. For example, if there are 4 Adventurers in the Campaign, the starting Heat is 4.

Heat rises by 1 when:

  • ♦ Point of No Return: At the end of a Point of No Return.

  • ♦ Sad Goodbye: When an Adventurer is Left for Dead, or when a supporting character meets their bitter end.

  • ♦ It’s Late: When the Adventurers waste time, or when they linger too long at Camp.

  • ♦ Caught: When the Adventurers get captured.

  • ♦ Supernatural Threat: The first time the Adventurers meet a supernatural creature.

  • ♦ Rival in the Lead: When the Rival or one of their goons gets the upper hand on the Adventurers, when they succeed in reaching a crucial objective, or get their hands on the Key.

Regardless of this, as soon as the Moment of Truth begins, the Heat stops rising until the end of the Campaign.

Camp

There is more to the life of an Adventurer than unexplored tombs, blades falling from the ceiling, and break-neck races in crumbling temples. Now and then, you need to take a break, catch your breath, and finish your day in a cozy Camp.

A Camp is a safe place where you can finally relax and focus on all those activities you don’t usually have enough time for during your hectic adventures. As the name suggests, Camps are usually composed of a handful of tents or improvised beds placed around a flickering fire. In some cases, however, Camp can be a metaphorical moment of peace, it could take place in a hotel when you're far from home, in the cargo hold of a plane, or in the cabin of a rickety fishing boat.

If you are using the rules from outgunned together with adventure, the Camp takes the place of the Time-Out.

Setting up Camp

You can set up Camp up to once per Session, and only if you have found a safe location, sheltered from the elements and protected from dangers.

If there are enemies or wild beasts nearby, the Director can declare that the location is not safe enough for you to set up Camp. When this happens, an Adventurer can give up their spot in the Camp to stand guard, allowing their friends to rest safely. Still, this should be an exception to the rule and it shouldn’t happen often.

When to set up Camp

The Camp is a necessary moment that allows all Adventurers to talk freely among themselves, recover their Grit, and get ready for what’s to come.

Even if you can potentially set up Camp at any moment, it’s usually best if you plan for this phase to take place at the very beginning or end of a Session. This will allow the Adventurers to get the best out of the moment of peace offered by a Camp, possibly reflecting on what has just happened, or refreshing their memories about the events of the previous Session.

If the Players feel like setting up Camp right in the middle of a Session, I recommend you set the Camp scene exactly at the center of your Shot, so that it won’t cause too much of a break in the pacing of the story.

What happens at Camp

When your group manages to set up Camp, you can finally breathe a sigh of relief and recover all lost Grit . You are safe, for now at least. You don’t need to worry about poisoned darts and other lurking dangers.

In mechanical terms, Camp works much like all other Scenes, the main difference is that it spans over a longer time, allowing each action to take up minutes, and sometimes even hours. You can finally focus on all those activities that take too long to fit into the grueling pace of your adventure, like tending to bad wounds, repairing a rifle, or puzzling out a clue.

Using Experiences

Experiences are like small custom-made Feats that can be called into play by either yourself or the Director in certain circumstances, and they can grant you Help or impose Hindrance at some point on your adventure (See Help & Hindrance in Section 2: BASICS OF THE GAME ).

For example, the Achievement “ I’ve learned to put my trust in others ” can grant a Help to rolls made to act in tandem with a friend. On the other hand, the Scar “ I was locked in a room full of spiders ” can become a Hindrance when you’re facing an arachnid.

Usually, Achievements will be of Help and Scars will become Hindrances, but that’s not always the case.

For example, the Achievement “ I’ve learned to put my trust in others ” may be a Hindrance to rolls made to understand whether someone is lying to you or plotting against you. On the other hand, the Scar “ I was locked in a room full of spiders ” can become a Help when you’re walking through a hallway crawling with insects. At least it’s not spiders!

Finally, Bonds and Reputations can be either an advantage or disadvantage, depending on the circumstances and people involved.

Preface

Erik the Red was possibly the greatest Viking explorer, and over his eventful life, he witnessed incredible things and accumulated unimaginable wealth.

At the beginning of the year 1000, he retreated to live in Greenland, a land he had discovered himself, and spend his last days like a true Viking, refusing to follow his son’s example and convert to Christianity.

The Shot opens with the Adventurers climbing a frozen cliff in Greenland as they search for the tomb of the legendary explorer, armed with nothing but their thirst for adventure and a mysterious amulet, Odin’s Key .

Days before, the Adventurers were hired by Professor Kasper Wieloch , a wealthy luminary who entrusted them with Odin’s Key and pointed them to a cliff in Greenland, asking them to retrieve a priceless and ancient artifact that should be located in the lost tomb of Erik the Red.

Not even Professor Wielock knows what the artifact is, but it’s pretty clear that he is willing to pay good money to any Adventurer who succeeds in retrieving it.

SCENE 1 - EXT. FROZEN MOUNTAIN - DAY

Greenland, 1936.

1 

The scene opens with the ADVENTURERS hanging on the steep wall of
a frozen mountain. They can barely glimpse the end of the climb
above them, while beneath, thick white clouds hide a terrifying
precipice. Nobody would survive such a fall.
Their only option is to keep climbing to reach their destination
with Odin’s Key.

The ADVENTURERS are tied to each other with a thick rope and must climb in single file, as ruthless winds try to yank them from their handholds.

2

The first in line is responsible for placing large pitons in the frozen wall to secure the rope, creating a safe holding point for the climbers coming after them. The character who volunteers to place the pitons must make an Action Roll in Brawn+Survival .

If they score a Critical Success or greater, they manage to firmly plant the piton in the ice, and the other ADVENTURERS can keep climbing without much trouble until they reach the mountain summit.

If the ADVENTURER only scores a Basic Success the piton creates a small but dangerous crack, which could open wider as the others put their weight onto it. Every ADVENTURER who uses that piton on their climb must flip a coin. If the result is Heads, the crack becomes too wide and the piton suddenly slips out. Finally, if the ADVENTURER who places the piton scores no Success at all , the piton gives way as soon as the next person hangs onto it.

3 

If the piton slips away, the ADVENTURER who was using it to climb
runs the risk of falling down, as does everyone behind them [Ba-
sic Reaction Roll  in Nerves+Dexterity] while all the ADVEN-
TURERS who had already climbed over the piton, including the one
who planted it, risk being dragged down by their allies [Basic
Reaction Roll  in Brawn+Endure].

After overcoming this possible hiccup, the ADVENTURERS can final - ly end their grueling climb and reach the summit. Once there, they can clearly see an imposing burial structure that looks like a small mausoleum nestled in the frozen landscape. Professor WIELOCH was right, the tomb of Erik the Red exists and they have found it.

SCENE 2 - EXT. TOMB ON THE SUMMIT - DAY

On top of the mountain, the wind is even harsher than during
the climb, carrying the threatening sound of waves that crash
hundreds of feet below. By looking over the edge on one side,
the ADVENTURERS can see the wall they just had to climb, while
on the other side, the sea spreads as far as the eye can see.
It’s appropriate for the tomb of a Viking explorer who ruled the
seas a thousand years ago to be located in such a beautiful and
unreachable place.

The tomb of Erik the Red is a classic Christian monument dating back to the year 1000, composed of a large memorial stone standing out against the sky. The huge gravestone is completely surrounded by ice, wedged deep, and decorated with a Christian cross and the inscription “ sicut vixi ego relinquo ” (As I lived, so I depart). An ADVENTURER can attempt to translate this inscription with a Basic Action Roll in Focus+Culture , but those who can’t rely on the Archeology Feat suffer -1 to the roll.

4 

As they step closer, the ADVENTURERS can easily notice a slot in
the tombstone shaped exactly like Odin’s Key, the artifact that
they are carrying.

The tomb of Erik the Red is actually a fake. An ingenious trap
designed by the Viking explorer to ensure that only his most
loyal followers could reach his treasure.

If the ADVENTURERS insert the Key into the slot, the tomb starts
to shake, provoking an actual earthquake that causes the moun-
tain summit to crumble, dragging the ADVENTURERS down the cliff
[Critical Reaction Roll  in Brawn+Stunt].

5

6 

After the dive, the ADVENTURERS reemerge from the freezing wa-
ters and can spot the entrance to a cave. Those who fail to score
at least a Basic Success also suffer the Frozen Condition.

If the ADVENTURERS wait before inserting the Key, they have sev-
eral ways to notice that the tomb is actually a trap.
They can either take the time to ponder the words carved in the
stone and the fact that the Key to the treasure was dedicated
to Odin, or they can make a Critical Action Roll in Focus+Know.
Either way, the ADVENTURERS can come to the realization that Erik
the Red never betrayed his pagan faith, and thus would  have
never accepted a Christian burial.

A true Viking such as Erik the Red certainly entrusted his mortal
spoils to the cold waters of the sea. So, if they look toward the
sea at the bottom of the cliff, the clever ADVENTURERS can spot
the entrance to a secret cave.

4

This Feels Too Easy

Once the climb is over, the Adventurers reach what looks like the
gravestone of Erik the Red, and everything appears to be going
according to plan. Try to extend this moment, giving the Adven-
turers some time to realize that their hunt for the treasure is
going all too well. You don’t need to give away the solution,
just pick your words carefully as you describe this perfectly
carved slot, shaped exactly like Odin’s Key, that the Adventur-
ers have found so easily, requiring no further effort and posing
no challenge.

5

Everything Falls Apart

If the Adventurers are reckless and don’t think twice before inserting Odin’s Key in the fake tomb, the ground will literally collapse under their feet. The consequences will be moments of sheer panic, some Dangerous Rolls, and a rough fall that ends with a hefty belly-flop in the freezing waters of Greenland. Lo and behold, this pitfall will bring them right to their target: such is the life of Adventurers!

6

Worship of Odin

Erik the Red never abandoned his faith and never accepted to con-
vert to Christianity. If any one of the Adventurers can remember
this information or deduce it thanks to the clues in the scene,
they have well earned 1 Luck. You should always reward knowledge!
Alternatively, the situation can also be resolved with a good old
dice roll in Focus+Know.

However, even if none of the Adventurers are experts in histo-
ry or religion, there are other ways to realize that this is a
trap. For example, they could examine the shape of the Key with
an Action Roll in Crime+Fix, or lean out over the edge to look
for a passage at the bottom of the cliff.

In any case, your job as a Director is to give the Adventur- ers all the tools they need to find a solution, and reward their bright ideas and intuitions.

SCENE 3 - INT. SECRET CAVE - DAY

After climbing down the cliff or plunging into the freezing waters, the ADVENTURERS reach the entrance of a sea cave. There, they find a path leading deep into the icy mountain.

The cave appears to be of a natural origin, but a Basic Action Roll can reveal that someone or something caused a collapse and significantly narrowed the entrance.

As they reach the depths of the cave, the temperature rises noticeably and the ADVENTURERS are surrounded by dark- ness. They must find a source of light in order to proceed. Lucky for them, there are some Viking skeletons on the ground, with their crumbling weapons and rotting torches.

An ADVENTURER can try to light one of the torches with a Critical Action Roll . Regardless of the roll’s outcome, the torch catches the flame and sheds light into the darkness. At least for now…

As they proceed on the path, the ADVENTURERS realize that the ice beneath their feet is slowly turning into a finished stone floor. Holding the torch high, they see the beginning of a man-made hallway dating back to the year 1000. They are on the correct path. 

At the end of the hallway, they can make out a stone statue of
Odin, the Norse god, holding a golden spear in one hand.
On the ground, the ADVENTURERS can see square tiles in many fad-
ing colors apparently laid without a regular pattern.

7 

Unfortunately for the ADVENTURERS, the hallway is a lethal trap.
Setting foot on the wrong tile could prove fatal for them.

By trying to examine the statue of Odin from a distance, the
ADVENTURERS can notice it wears a golden eyepatch over its left
eye. And on the patch, there are runes that look similar – ac-
tually, identical – to the ones on the Odin’s Key they carry.
In order to make it past the trap, an ADVENTURER must place
Odin’s Key in front of their eye and look through the stone as
if it were a monocle. Only then can they see the right path to
follow in order to avoid triggering the trap.

Odin’s Route

Erik the Red wanted to ensure that only a true Viking could make
it past his traps, and this is why Odin’s Route hides a clev-
er catch!

If an Adventurer looks through Odin’s Key with their left eye
(the same side covered by the golden eyepatch in the statue) they
will easily spot a path on the tiles… but alas, it will be the
wrong one! It will only take three steps, barely enough to draw
a sigh of relief, before the trap triggers.

Historically, Odin always wears a patch on his right eye . Erik the Red placed it wrong on the statue to mislead future grave-robbers! The Adventurers can see through this trick thanks to their own knowledge, or by making a roll in Focus+Know at the right time. Always reward players for their intuition and caution, maybe with a nice Spotlight! By looking through Odin’s Key with their right eye, an Adventurer can finally find the right path through the hallway .

Stupid Torch!

If the Adventurer who lit the torch failed their roll, this is
the perfect time to teach our explorer friends about the conse-
quences of their mistakes!

Take your time as you describe the flame on the torch that starts to waver, slowly grows dim, and ultimately goes out, plunging them into darkness.

Disarming the Trap

In Outgunned Adventure failure is never an option, so the Ad- venturers will always find a way to disarm the trap and get out of the thorny situation. But this doesn’t mean that everything will go their way! 

As consequence for a failure when disarming the trap, you can say
that the Adventurers will need to face another Reaction Roll ,
that they are forced to look for a different solution, or that
they will suffer a Condition before making it through. It’s up
to you!

According to history books, Erik the Red sailed toward the coast of Greenland with 25 ships, but only 14 of those returned. SCENE 5 - INT. VIKING SHIP - DAY

On one side, the bow of the ship is deeply wedged into the ice. On the other, the stern is gingerly resting on a rock spur. The entirety of the Viking drakkar is suspended in the void, and could fall into the chasm at any moment.

Maybe a thousand years ago this chasm was filled with water. What’s certain, is that the ADVENTURERS won’t have an easy time climbing aboard given the current conditions. 

This Scene is divided in 4 main phases, but it’s extremely
freeform, and there are several ways to go about it.

1. The ADVENTURERS need to get on the ship . For example, they could use ropes and grappling hooks, build an improvised bridge, or climb on one of the ice walls. Depending on their approach, they will need to make Basic or Critical rolls.

2. Once on the ship, the ADVENTURERS must find a way to get below deck and reach the inner hold door. In this phase, they must choose their moves with extreme caution, possibly making Stealth rolls to avoid compromising the ship’s delicate balance.

3. Below deck, the ADVENTURERS find a heavy trapdoor that can be forced or lockpicked through a Critical Action Roll .

4. In the cargo hold of the ship, the ADVENTURERS find nothing by old crates full of frozen fish and other trivial items. In order to proceed, they must find the secret passage to the treasure of Erik the Red . 

The problem is that the Viking ship is extremely unstable.
Whenever the ADVENTURERS fail a roll, the ship will roll to one
side as a consequence, possibly even turning upside down, get-
ting ever-closer to the point when it falls into the chasm.

Whenever the ship shifts, all ADVENTURERS must face a Critical
Reaction Roll and run the risk of losing some Grit.
Additionally, when the ship is on one side or upside-down, the
ADVENTURERS could lose their bearings and be forced to climb
back to the hold instead of continuing their descent, as well as
having to deal with all problems that could follow.

Stop the Ship, I Want to Get Off!

The massive Viking drakkar suspended on an icy chasm that rolls over with every misstep is certainly a focal point for this adventure! Have fun describing the dangers and absurdities of this peculiar Scene. If you’re ever in doubt, you can roll a die or use one of these ideas:

1. The ship slips to its left side.

  • Ice spikes fall from the wall above, falling toward the ship. Roll Nerves+Stealth to dodge.

2. The ship swings to its right side. Crates, barrels, and wooden benches come toward you at full speed. Roll Crime+Awareness to dodge.

3. The ship slips lower and comes to a sudden stop. For a moment, everything seems lost. Roll Nerves+Cool. In the end, you’re all okay!

4. The ship turns upside-down. You get hurled upwards and smack straight onto the ceiling. Roll Brawn+Stunt to land safely. Now everything is upside-down.

5. The ship falls several feet and only the bow remains stuck. After a moment of panic, you find yourselves dangling by the bow like a pendulum. Roll Brawn+Endure to keep holding on. Things are even more unstable now.

6. The ship spins on itself and returns right-side up. Everything is reeling. Roll Nerves+Dexterity to grab onto something before it’s too late.

If an Adventurer fills in their Bad Box during this Scene, you can choose whether they suffer the Hurt, Nervous, or Scared Condition.

SCENE 6 - INT. TREASURE HOLD - DAY

The hold of the ship is full of crates and rotting barrels, but
there’s no trace of a treasure or anything valuable left by Erik
the Red. Looking around, it appears as if all their searching led
to a hole in the water, but the Viking ship is obviously hiding
one last secret: a hidden false bottom.

The ADVENTURERS can find the false bottom leading to the treasure hold by moving some of the planks in the floor, or by making a successful roll in Focus+Detect. Alternatively, when a failure causes the ship to roll for the first time, a large crack forms in the wood, causing old and worn gold coins to scatter through the hold and revealing the path to the treasure.

Waiting for them at the bottom of the hold, there’s the skeleton

Fortune and Glory?

Such a huge treasure is bound to make an impression. If the Adventurers are in bad shape (for example, if most of them have already filled in their Bad Box), this could be the perfect chance to allow them to Catch a Break . 

Anyway, not all that glitters is gold, because if the Adven-
turers try to stuff their pockets with even a small part of the
treasure, they shift the weight of the coins and start a chain
reaction that will plummet the ship into the gorge. They’ll have
to hurry, get the disk, and run.

Have fun describing how a simple, tiny, innocuous coin can throw
the entire cargo off balance: this is a classic!

So this is what Professor Wieloch was looking for, a map to At-

Why Does Everything Need to Collapse?!

Either due to their own greed or to a twist of Fate, the Adven-

main mast to reach a frozen wall, or, why not, even improvise a
parachute using the ship’s sails and the warm air that blows from
the deep. The crazier their plan, the more epic their escape!
